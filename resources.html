Resources

The data behind these guides comes from Australian government monitoring, independent research, and advocacy organisations. Here's what we used and where to go next.

Government

APVMA — Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority ↗
Federal regulator · Approval & review data
The federal body responsible for approving and reviewing agricultural chemicals in Australia. Its November 2025 decision to suspend dimethoate products for use on blueberries, raspberries and blackberries is cited directly in the Dirty Dozen guide.

FSANZ Australian Total Diet Study (ATDS) ↗
Food Standards Australia New Zealand · Government monitoring
The primary Australian government program that measures dietary exposure to pesticide residues. Analyses a market basket of commonly eaten foods to estimate actual consumer exposure. Used as the baseline for Australian-specific residue data across all three guides.

National Residue Survey (NRS) ↗
Australian Department of Agriculture · Government monitoring
Government program monitoring chemical residues in Australian agricultural produce, primarily for export compliance purposes. Provides data on what AU-grown produce carries at the farm gate, complementing the ATDS market basket data.

Independent research and testing

CHOICE Australia ↗
CHOICE · Consumer advocacy
Australia's leading independent consumer organisation. CHOICE has conducted and published investigations into pesticide residues in Australian-bought produce, including testing of imported garlic and other high-risk items, and its 2008 test of strawberries from 31 growers (published with an explainer on residues and maximum residue limits in 2014). Used to supplement government data with independent consumer-focused findings.

Friends of the Earth Melbourne — The Dose Makes the Poison (2012) ↗
Friends of the Earth Melbourne · compiled by Anthony Amis · Independent research
A landmark independent analysis of 12 years of FSANZ ATDS data (2000–2011) identifying which produce items carried the highest detection rates and chemical loads in Australia. One of the few systematic AU-specific rankings available and a foundational source for the Dirty Dozen and Tread Carefully.

Pesticide Action Australia — Australia's Residue Report 2026 ↗
Pesticide Action Australia · Advocacy and independent testing
The peak body advocating for the reduction of pesticide use in Australia. PAA monitors government policy, supports independent research, and campaigns for safer food systems. Their 2026 report with independent testing has helped us immensely in confirming the data in these guides.

Southern Cross University berry testing (2025), reported by SBS News ↗
Southern Cross University · Independent testing by Prof. Kirsten Benkendorff · Link: SBS News coverage · Dirty Dozen specific
2025 research from Southern Cross University finding dimethoate residues in Australian blueberries at levels that could exceed safe daily intake estimates if eaten daily. A key piece of AU-specific evidence cited in the Dirty Dozen blueberry entry and context for the APVMA suspension proposal. NSW’s EPA and Food Authority later said their testing found no evidence of thiometon, the other (banned) chemical reported, and no evidence that blueberries are unsafe to eat. The dimethoate findings and the APVMA’s suspension stand separately.

Industry

Hort Innovation — Residue and production data (2025)
Hort Innovation · Industry body · Tread Carefully specific
Australia's horticulture industry research and development body. Hort Innovation data on production practices and residue profiles for specific vegetable and citrus categories informs context in the Tread Carefully guide, particularly for snow peas and domestic growing practices.

International and media

EWG Shopper's Guide to Pesticides in Produce (2026) ↗
Environmental Working Group (US) · Annual report
The US Environmental Working Group's annual analysis of USDA pesticide testing data, producing the widely-cited Dirty Dozen and Clean Fifteen lists. Used in these guides as international comparative data and specifically for chemicals such as PFAS (bifenthrin, fludioxonil) where AU-specific data is absent. US rankings are not directly applicable to Australia but provide relevant context where AU data gaps exist.

The Guardian Australia — FOI investigation ↗
The Guardian AU · Investigative journalism · FOI documents
Freedom of Information documents obtained by The Guardian Australia revealing an unpublished 2013 pilot study by the federal agriculture department, including detections of fenthion at unacceptable levels in peaches and dimethoate up to 90× the MRL in strawberries (2013 data). This reporting uncovered data the government had not proactively disclosed.

Washing and preparation

APVMA product label: Deccozil EC Fungicide (imazalil) ↗
Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority · Registered product label
A registered postharvest fungicide for citrus, apples and pears, with directions for mixing it into the packing wax. It backs our tip to scrub citrus before zesting.
What can I do
Better Health Channel: Food, pesticides and other chemicals ↗
Victorian Government · Consumer health advice
Advises washing all fruit and vegetables thoroughly, organic included, alongside buying organic and growing your own.
What can I do
CSIRO: Listeria in rockmelons, what you need to know (2018) ↗
CSIRO · Food safety explainer
Explains how bacteria on a rockmelon’s skin can be carried into the flesh when it’s sliced, and why a scrub before cutting does no harm.
What can I do
Cunningham, Steciuk & Taverner (2007): Imazalil in wax on late-picked navel oranges ↗
Australasian Postharvest Conference · Conference paper
Notes that Australian citrus packers routinely add fungicide to the wax coating as an insurance policy against mould in storage and transit.
What can I do
de Montagnac et al. (2026): Reducing pesticide residues on produce: a scoping review of household produce washing methods ↗
Frontiers in Environmental Health · Peer-reviewed scoping review
A review of 47 studies comparing rinsing, soaking, and bi-carb and vinegar soaks. The source of our "around a third" and "about half" figures, the 20-second rinse and the 1:3 vinegar mix. Led by a scientist at the Environmental Working Group.
What can I do
Keikotlhaile, Spanoghe & Steurbaut (2010): Effects of food processing on pesticide residues in fruits and vegetables ↗
Food and Chemical Toxicology · Peer-reviewed meta-analysis
Combines results across studies to compare washing, peeling, blanching and boiling. Supports our points on peeling and cooking.
What can I do
NSW Food Authority: Fresh cut fruit and vegetables ↗
NSW Government · Food safety fact sheet
Advises washing produce in clean water just before preparing it, peeling or removing outer leaves where appropriate, and cutting away damaged parts.
What can I do
Yang et al. (2017): Effectiveness of commercial and homemade washing agents in removing pesticide residues on and in apples ↗
Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry · Peer-reviewed study
Found a 1% bi-carb soak removed more surface residue than tap water or a commercial bleach wash, and showed one fungicide had penetrated 80 micrometres into the fruit. The basis of our bi-carb ratio.
